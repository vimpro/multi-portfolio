Relevant topics: parametrics, polar form, conic sections

This unit was about parametrics, polar equations, and conic sections. It is sensical in many ways to cover these concepts in the same unit, especially the former two because they are extensions beyond the traditional Cartesian coordinate system. Parametric and polar form are are partiuclarly useful because they allow you to express curves that aren't expressable in the Cartesian form.

Problem 1: parametrics

Let's take a look at a parametrics problem first. An example is the cycloid curve:

To solve for the position of the given point on the wheel as a function of time, I first described the motion in the vertical direction. If we ignore the horizontal movement, the vertical motion is simply a sinusoid (negated, because the motion of the wheel is clockwise), shifted up by 'r' (since the center of the wheel is a height of 'r' above the ground):

\[ y(t) = -rsin(t) + r \]

From there, the horizontal motion consists of two components: a constant velocity to the right, and a sinusoidal component left and right. Since velocity is distance * time, this is the result:

\[ x(t) = rcos(t) + rt \]

This technique of using a parametric is super useful not only because we can separate the horizontal and vertical components of the motion, but also because it is literally required for some problems like this one. There isn't actually a way to write the cartesian equation at all, since 't' is both a polynomial and a trigonometric term.
